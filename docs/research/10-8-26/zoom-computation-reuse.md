Architectural Strategies for Cross-Frame Computational Reuse in High-Precision Mandelbrot Zoom RenderingGenerating deep, high-resolution zoom animations of the Mandelbrot set presents a formidable computational challenge. At extreme magnification depths—frequently exceeding scale factors of $10^{100}$ or $10^{1000}$—standard IEEE-754 double-precision floating-point hardware suffers from fatal underflow, necessitating arbitrary-precision arithmetic engines such as MPFR or GMP. Evaluating every frame of a video sequence independently under arbitrary precision requires computing thousands to millions of complex polynomial iterations per pixel, creating an unsustainable rendering bottleneck.To make deep zoom animation practical, specialized graphics software relies on cross-frame computational reuse. Rather than treating each frame as an isolated computation, these systems exploit spatial, temporal, and coordinate-space redundancies inherent in known-path zoom sequences. This report provides an exhaustive technical analysis of four core paradigms for cross-frame computation reuse: exponential-map (log-polar strip) keyframe rendering pipelines, keyframe-plus-interpolation perturbation frameworks, and analogues drawn from real-time graphics including temporal reprojection, motion-compensated prediction, and virtual texturing clipmaps.Exponential-Map and Log-Polar Strip RenderingExponential-map rendering—also known as log-polar or Mercator-Mandelbrot projection—accelerates zoom video production by transforming concentric exponential rings around a fixed zoom center $c_0 \in \mathbb{C}$ into rectangular image strips. Standard Cartesian complex coordinates $c$ are mapped to log-polar coordinates $w = x + i y$ according to the conformal transformation:$$w = \ln(c - c_0)$$$$c(x,y) = c_0 + e^{x + i y}$$In this unwrapped coordinate space, the real component $x = \ln\vert{}c - c_0\vert{}$ measures the logarithmic radial distance (zoom scale depth) relative to $c_0$, while the imaginary component $y = \arg(c - c_0) \in [0, 2\pi)$ corresponds to the angular phase surrounding the zoom center. Concentric circles around $c_0$ in the Cartesian plane transform into vertical lines on the log-polar strip, whereas radial rays emanating from $c_0$ transform into horizontal lines.In practical rendering implementations such as Kalle's Fraktaler 2+ (KF) and Fraktaler 3, exponential map keyframes are rendered as rectangular strips with ultra-wide aspect ratios—typically 9:1, such as $1152 \times 128$, $4608 \times 512$, or $12288 \times 1365$ pixels. Keyframe sequences are exported in OpenEXR (.exr) format containing raw floating-point calculation layers, including integer escape iterations ($N$), fractional iteration offsets ($NF$), complex phase ($T$), running derivatives ($J_{xa}, J_{xb}, J_{ya}, J_{yb}$), and directional distance estimates ($DEX, DEY$).A dedicated video assembler, such as zoomasm, processes these OpenEXR keyframe strips. Rather than evaluating complex fractal polynomials for every output video frame, zoomasm uses GPU fragment shaders to execute continuous inverse coordinate reprojection in real time. For any output frame at zoom scale $Z(t)$, each target screen pixel $(u,v)$ maps back to a specific coordinate $(x,y)$ on the cached OpenEXR strip keyframes, executing dynamic palette lookup, analytic distance estimation, and slope lighting entirely within the GPU shading pipeline.In traditional Cartesian frame-by-frame rendering, generating a video of length $F$ frames at a screen resolution of $W \times H$ requires evaluating $O(F \cdot W \cdot H \cdot \bar{N})$ complex polynomial iterations, where $\bar{N}$ is the average iteration count per pixel. Exponential-map rendering decouples video frame generation from iteration evaluation. Keyframe strips are calculated only at discrete logarithmic scale intervals—typically at step scale factors of $\Delta Z_\text{key} = 2.0$ (one keyframe strip per scale doubling). The total number of iteration-heavy keyframes $K$ depends strictly on the total exponential depth of the video, completely independent of the output video frame rate:$$K = \frac{\ln(Z_\text{total})}{\ln(\Delta Z_\text{key})}$$For a zoom sequence spanning $T_\text{vid}$ seconds at frame rate $f_\text{fps}$, standard rendering evaluates $F = T_\text{vid} \cdot f_\text{fps}$ complete frames, whereas exponential-map rendering evaluates only $K$ log-polar strips. The speedup factor $\eta_\text{exp}$ relative to brute-force flat rendering is expressed as:$$\eta_\text{exp} \approx \frac{F \cdot A_\text{flat}}{K \cdot A_\text{strip}} = \frac{T_\text{vid} \cdot f_\text{fps} \cdot (W_\text{flat} \cdot H_\text{flat})}{\left( \frac{\ln Z_\text{total}}{\ln \Delta Z_\text{key}} \right) \cdot (W_\text{strip} \cdot H_\text{strip})}$$Because a typical log-polar keyframe strip ($1152 \times 128 \approx 147,456$ pixels) contains substantially fewer pixels than a standard Full HD video frame ($1920 \times 1080 \approx 2,073,600$ pixels) while covering a complete $2\times$ zoom octave, and because dozens of intermediate video frames are interpolated per keyframe octave, exponential-map rendering routinely reduces total iteration workloads by $95\%$ to over $99.9\%$. As zoom speed slows (increasing video frame count $F$ relative to zoom depth), the computational savings per frame increase linearly.Handling camera movement in log-polar space depends on whether the motion is rotational or translational. Rotation around the fixed zoom center $c_0$ translates directly to a linear vertical offset along the imaginary axis ($y$-axis) of the log-polar strip. Because the complex exponential mapping is periodic along the imaginary axis with period $2\pi$ ($\exp(x + i(y + 2\pi)) = \exp(x + iy)$), continuous camera rotation is accomplished by wrapping $y \pmod{2\pi}$ inside the GPU reprojection shader without executing a single additional fractal iteration. Conversely, panning (translating the zoom center $c_0 \to c_0 + \Delta c$) breaks the conformal radial symmetry surrounding $c_0$. Moving away from $c_0$ warps straight lines in the reprojected view and displaces the logarithmic singularity away from the center of the viewport. Consequently, log-polar strip architectures require a fixed target point $c_0$ per keyframe sequence. Off-center panning paths must be rendered by recalculating updated reference centers or generating secondary keyframe paths.Resampling distortion in exponential-map pipelines primarily arises from spatial scale disparities between the log-polar grid and the Cartesian screen grid. Near the screen center ($c \to c_0$), $x = \ln\vert{}c - c_0\vert{} \to -\infty$. Because a finite log-polar strip truncates rendering at a minimum cutoff radius $x_\text{min}$, reprojecting screen pixels closer to $c_0$ forces the shader to magnify the leftmost pixel columns of the strip, resulting in central pixelation, stretching, or blurring. Conversely, near the outer viewport edges, screen pixels sparsely sample the strip, which can induce spatial aliasing and Moiré patterns on fine fractal filaments. Practitioners mitigate these artefacts by rendering keyframe strips with subframe jittering, higher antialiasing supersampling rates ($N_s > 1$), or expanded keyframe strip resolutions ($12288 \times 1365$). Visual quality is evaluated by checking the continuity of analytic distance estimation gradients and verifying central sharpness prior to keyframe transitions.Exact sample positions are not shared between Cartesian video frames and log-polar keyframes. Screen pixels lie on a uniform Cartesian lattice, whereas keyframe samples reside on an exponential polar grid. Every output pixel requires dynamic spatial reconstruction filtering (such as bilinear, bicubic, or shader-level supersampling) across neighboring log-polar texels.Keyframe-Plus-Interpolation PipelinesKeyframe-plus-interpolation pipelines operating directly in Cartesian space rely on Perturbation Theory and Series Approximation (such as Bilinear Linear Approximation, or BLA) to accelerate deep zoom calculations. Standard escape-time algorithms compute a full sequence $z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c$ using arbitrary precision for every pixel. Under Perturbation Theory, a single central reference point $C$ is chosen, and its high-precision reference orbit $Z_n$ is evaluated and stored in memory:$$Z_{n+1} = Z_n^2 + C$$For any nearby pixel $c = C + \Delta c$, its orbit $z_n = Z_n + \Delta z_n$ is computed by iterating only the low-precision delta offset $\Delta z_n$ using standard hardware floating-point math:$$\Delta z_{n+1} = 2 Z_n \Delta z_n + \Delta z_n^2 + \Delta c$$In keyframe-interpolation architectures, the high-precision reference orbit $Z_n$ calculated at keyframe $k$ is cached and reused across consecutive video frames $k+1, k+2, \dots, k+m$, provided the active camera viewport remains within the valid perturbation radius of $C$.The computational cost of deep Mandelbrot rendering is heavily dominated by the high-precision reference orbit calculation, which scales as $O(N \cdot M(P))$, where $N$ is the maximum iteration count, $P$ is the required precision in bits, and $M(P)$ is the cost of $P$-bit multiplication. Evaluating low-precision pixel deltas $\Delta z_n$ requires only hardware single- or double-precision operations scaling as $O(N \cdot W \cdot H)$. Reusing a precomputed reference orbit $Z_n$ across $F_\text{reuse}$ consecutive video frames amortizes the setup cost $C_\text{ref}$ according to:$$\text{Compute Saved per Frame} = C_\text{ref} \left( 1 - \frac{1}{F_\text{reuse}} \right)$$As zoom speed slows, the camera viewport spends more frames within the validity boundary of $C$, maximizing $F_\text{reuse}$. At extreme zoom depths such as $10^{500}$ ($P \approx 2000$ bits), evaluating $Z_n$ requires several seconds to minutes of CPU/GPU time, whereas hardware float delta iterations execute in milliseconds. Reusing $Z_n$ yields frame-rate speedups of $50\times$ to over $1000\times$ compared to computing new reference orbits every frame. Incorporating Bilinear Linear Approximation (BLA) enables the renderer to skip thousands of linear iteration steps whenever $\vert{}\Delta z_n\vert{}$ remains small, yielding additional order-of-magnitude acceleration.Spatial camera transformations under keyframe perturbation pipelines affect pixel displacement vectors $\Delta c$ relative to the reference point $C$. Rotating the viewport by angle $\theta$ requires multiplying 2D complex offset vectors by $e^{i\theta}$ prior to delta iteration ($\Delta c_\text{rotated} = e^{i\theta} \cdot \Delta c_\text{local}$), requiring zero recalculation of the high-precision reference orbit $Z_n$. Panning shifts the viewport center away from $C$, increasing the delta magnitude $\vert{}\Delta c\vert{}$. When $\vert{}\Delta z_n\vert{}$ grows too large relative to $\vert{}Z_n\vert{}$, the perturbation approximation breaks down. This failure triggers a rebasing operation: selecting a new reference point $C_\text{new}$, evaluating a new high-precision reference orbit, or shifting the iteration index $m_n$.Perturbation keyframe pipelines do not experience resampling blur, but they are vulnerable to numerical breakdown artefacts known as glitches. Glitches manifest as prominent blocks or patches of solid, incorrect color resulting from catastrophic precision loss during floating-point cancellation. Furthermore, reusing a reference orbit whose period or inflection behavior does not match the local minibrot structure introduces false structural distortions. Practitioners monitor rendering quality using automated glitch-detection passes that scan output frames for spatial iteration step discontinuities. If detected glitch pixels exceed a strict tolerance threshold (such as $>0.01\%$ of total frame pixels), the system automatically triggers a reference orbit rebase.Regarding sample position sharing, keyframe perturbation pipelines share exact reference orbit arrays ($Z_n$) across hundreds of video frames. However, because the camera viewport rescales continuously, individual subpixel delta offsets $\Delta c$ vary from frame to frame, requiring pixel delta iterations to be re-evaluated fresh for each unique viewport position.Analogues from Real-Time Games and Video GraphicsTemporal Reprojection and Temporal Anti-AliasingIn real-time rendering, Temporal Anti-Aliasing (TAA) and temporal reprojection reuse calculated pixel colors across consecutive frames by reprojecting historical frame buffers using screen-space velocity vectors. For a camera zooming into a fixed center $c_0$ at scale ratio $s_t = Z(t)/Z(t-1)$, a pixel at screen coordinate $\mathbf{p}_t = (x_t, y_t)$ in frame $t$ maps to a coordinate $\mathbf{p}_{t-1}$ in frame $t-1$ via the affine transformation:$$\mathbf{p}_{t-1} = c_0 + \frac{1}{s_t} (\mathbf{p}_t - c_0)$$The proportion of screen area in frame $t$ that overlaps with frame $t-1$'s viewport is defined by the inverse square ratio:$$\text{Area Overlap Ratio} = \frac{1}{s_t^2}$$At slow zoom speeds ($s_t \approx 1.01$ to $1.05$), the overlap ratio ranges from $90.7\%$ to $98.0\%$, enabling up to $98\%$ of screen pixels to be reprojected from the previous frame buffer, requiring fresh fractal evaluations for only $2\%$ to $9\%$ of new border pixels. However, at rapid zoom speeds ($s_t \ge 1.414$, or $>2\times$ scale per second), the overlap ratio drops below $50\%$, causing computational savings to degrade rapidly.Panning and rotation are incorporated by updating the $3 \times 3$ planar homography matrix $\mathbf{M}_{t \to t-1}$:$$\mathbf{p}_{t-1} = \mathbf{R}(-\theta_t) \cdot \left( \frac{1}{s_t} (\mathbf{p}_t - c_0) \right) - \mathbf{v}_\text{pan}$$Temporal reprojection introduces visible artefacts including temporal ghosting, trailing streaks, and high-frequency shimmering near intricate fractal boundary lines. Practitioners evaluate quality using Structural Similarity (SSIM), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), and temporal variance metrics across frame sequences. Exact sample positions are not shared due to subpixel jittering schemes, requiring bilinear or bicubic reconstruction filtering of historical buffers.Motion-Compensated PredictionDerived from video compression standards (such as H.264, HEVC, and AV1), motion-compensated prediction models frame $t$ by applying a 2D optical flow field $\vec{v}(x,y)$ to frame $t-1$. For a camera zooming toward $c_0$, the optical flow forms a radial velocity field:$$\vec{v}(r) = r \cdot \left( 1 - \frac{1}{s_t} \right) \hat{r}$$Where $r$ represents distance from $c_0$ and $\hat{r}$ is the radial unit vector.Motion compensation substitutes pixel iteration evaluations with block-wise dynamic texture transfers. At slow zoom speeds ($s_t \to 1.0$), motion vector prediction achieves $80\%$ to $90\%$ reductions in shading compute. As zoom velocity increases, prediction residual error grows, forcing the pipeline to recompute fractal iterations across expanding macroblock regions.Panning adds a uniform translation vector $\vec{v}_\text{trans}$ across the field, while rotation introduces an angular velocity component $\vec{v}_\text{rot}(r, \theta) = \omega \cdot r \cdot \hat{\theta}$:$$\vec{v}_\text{total} = r \left(1 - \frac{1}{s_t}\right) \hat{r} + \omega r \hat{\theta} + \vec{v}_\text{trans}$$Visible artefacts include macroblock grid boundary noise, edge tearing, and loss of subpixel fractal detail. Practitioners evaluate prediction efficiency using Rate-Distortion Optimization (RDO) criteria, Video Multi-Method Assessment Fusion (VMAF), and edge-preservation indices. Exact sample positions are not shared because continuous radial velocity fields map integer pixel grids in frame $t$ to fractional coordinates in frame $t-1$.Virtual Texturing and ClipmapsVirtual texturing and geometry clipmaps maintain a multi-resolution, hierarchical pyramid of cached texture tiles in GPU memory. Concentric clipmap rings surround the target center, with each ring representing a discrete mipmap zoom octave ($2^0, 2^1, 2^2, \dots, 2^k$).Virtual texturing decouples frame rendering from fractal iteration. The display loop executes simple quad texture sampling at $O(1)$ complexity per frame. Computational workload shifts entirely to asynchronous background tile generation threads. When zooming at speed $s$ octaves per second, the required tile calculation rate $R_\text{tile}$ scales as:$$R_\text{tile} = s \cdot N_\text{tiles\_per\_octave}$$If camera zoom speed $s$ exceeds asynchronous tile rendering capacity, the system temporarily displays lower-resolution tiles from higher clipmap rings until high-resolution tiles complete processing. Consequently, video frame rates remain stable regardless of fractal depth or iteration counts.Panning slides the clipmap rings horizontally or vertically across the coordinate space, evicting out-of-bounds tiles and streaming in newly exposed border tiles. Rotation is handled by applying UV coordinate rotations during quad rasterization, incurring zero extra tile compute.Dominant artefacts include Level-of-Detail (LOD) popping transitions and temporary mipmap blurring during rapid camera acceleration. Practitioners evaluate quality by monitoring texel-to-pixel scale ratios (targeting an ideal ratio of $1.0$), tile cache hit rates, and LOD transition latency. Keyframe sequences are initially generated using high-precision Perturbation Theory and Bilinear Linear Approximation engines inside applications such as Kalle's Fraktaler 2+ or Fraktaler 3. By evaluating keyframes as 2D log-polar strips exported at discrete $2\times$ scale octaves in OpenEXR format, computation times for extreme deep zooms ($>10^{500}$) are reduced from months to hours.In the second stage, video assemblers such as zoomasm ingest the OpenEXR keyframes and apply OpenCL or OpenGL fragment shaders to perform real-time coordinate reprojection, color palette mapping, distance estimation, and directional slope lighting. This decoupling allows video timeline speeds, camera rotations, and audio-synchronized zoom accelerations to be adjusted dynamically without requiring additional fractal iteration calculations. Finally, shader-level subframe antialiasing and shutter-speed motion blur are synthesized during reprojection, suppressing spatial Moiré patterns and frame-to-frame shimmering to produce pristine fractal zoom videos.
